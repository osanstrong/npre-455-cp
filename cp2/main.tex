Consider the two-material infinite slab reactor of \figref{p1_setup}, in which a slab with a distributed source is surrounded symmetrically by two moderating slabs.
We aim to predict the neutron flux in this reactor, both spatially and at different energies, using multigroup theory and both analytical and numerical solutions.
\subsection{Theory}
In order to predict the neutron flux in the slabs, one could make use of the neutron diffusion model in Eq.~\ref{eq:generalneutrondiffusion}.
This model has been previously derived; it assumes linear anisotropy of neutron flux, isotropic neutron scattering, and ignores the distribution of neutrons in angle and energy (we additionally ignore delayed neutrons from fission, but this is for simplicity, as opposed to a drastic simplification of the model).
\newcommand{\pd}[3]{\frac{\partial^{#3}#1}{\partial{#2}^{#3}}}
% %This makes a new command '\pd' that takes in three arguments. The first is what you're applying the derivative to, the second is the independent variable, and the third is the order. An example usage is \pd{T}{x}{}, which will result in dT/dx or \pd{T}{x}{2} which will result in d^2T/dx^2
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{v}\pd{\phi}{t}{} - \nabla\cdot D \nabla\phi + \Sigma_a\phi = 
    % \Sigma_s\phi + 
    \nu\Sigma_f\phi+S
    \label{eq:generalneutrondiffusion}
\end{equation}
However, in this report we seek to include information about the energies of neutrons.
The distribution of material properties, and subsequently of neutron flux, vary too wildly with energy to reasonably integrate into this model as a continuous energy process (as opposed to Monte Carlo methods, where calculations for a single neutron could feasibly sample near-continuous property information).
Rather, a typical approach involves \emph{multigroup theory}, in which neutrons are categorized into some finite $G$ groups of energy, finely enough that within each group $g$ properties are self-consistent:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{v}\pd{\phi_g}{t}{} - \nabla\cdot D_g\nabla\phi_g + \Sigma_{ag}\phi_g + \sum_{g'\neq g}^{G}\Sigma_{sg\rightarrow g'}\phi_g=\sum_{g'\neq g}^{G}\Sigma_{sg'\rightarrow g}\phi_{g'} + \chi_g\nu\sum_{g'}^{G}{\Sigma_{fg'}\phi_g'} + S_g
    \label{eq:multigroup-diff}
\end{equation}
\newcommand{\outscat}{\Sigma_{sg\rightarrow g'}}
\newcommand{\inscat}{\Sigma_{sg'\rightarrow g}}
where the ``outscattering'' cross-section $\outscat$ represents the macroscopic cross-section for neutrons in energy group $g$ to scatter into an energy of group $g'$, and the ``inscattering'' cross-section $\inscat$ vice versa, from $g'$ to $g$.
These groups are typically numbered starting from the fastest group $g=1$ to the slowest energy group $g=G$.\\
\newcommand{\SigRem}{\Sigma_{Rg}}

The absorption $\Sigma_{ag}$ and outscattering cross-sections both apply to the same group's flux $\phi_g$, and are often combined into one ``removal'' cross-section $\SigRem$:\\
\begin{equation}
    \SigRem = \Sigma_{ag} + \sum_{g'\neq g}^{G}
    \label{eq:removal}
\end{equation}

To select groups of energy across with properties are \emph{similar} would still require an impractical number of groups, and so such groups are defined such that they are \emph{self-consistent}:
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_g\phi_g = \int_{E_g}^{E_{g+1}}\Sigma(E)\phi(E)dE
    \label{eq:consistent-gxs}
\end{equation}
Thus, knowing the energy spectrum of a comparable existing system, one could define properties for energy groups by integrating over that section:
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_g=\frac{\int_{E_g}^{E_{g+1}}\Sigma(E)\phi(E)dE}{\int_{E_g}^{E_{g+1}}\phi(E)dE}
    \label{eq:solve-gxs}
\end{equation}
This yields, as seen in Eq.~\ref{eq:multigroup-diff} a diffusion equation for each group which can be solved with two boundary conditions, as for the single group model.
However, since inscattering depends on every other energy's flux distribution, the flux distributions $\phi_g$ depend on all other distributions $\phi_{g'}$, producing tightly coupled diffusion equations with a very involved solution process.
Instead, an assumption is often made that there is no ``upscattering'', that is, neutrons never scatter from a slower group to a faster group $g\rightarrow g',g>g'$.
Since neutrons are typically far higher energy than those of atomic nuclei, this assumption is fairly viable unless dealing with multiple groups at such low, ``thermal'' energies that the thermal energy of atomic nuclei is comparable.
This makes the fastest flux distribution $\phi_1$ independent of the rest, allowing it to be solved first using typical boundary conditions.
The next flux distribution $\phi_2$, then, only depending on the single higher energy group $\phi_1$, can then use the $\phi_1$ solution and be solved itself, and so on and so forth until either the slowest group or reaching multiple thermal groups.
\subsection{Analytical Solution}
Consider, for example, the previously discussed system in \figref{p1_setup}.
Let us perform a multigroup diffusion analysis (assuming steady state operation) with two energy groups, and assume no upscattering from 2 to 1.
This yields two flux distributions to solve for using diffusion theory:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        - \nabla\cdot D_1\nabla\phi_1 + (\Sigma_{a1}+\Sigma_{s1\rightarrow 2})\phi_1= S_1\\
        - \nabla\cdot D_2\nabla\phi_2 + \Sigma_{a2}\phi_2= \Sigma_{1\rightarrow2}\phi_1 + S_2\\
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:multigroup-diff-nospace}
\end{equation}
Considering the one-dimensional, two-region problem, for which the source $S$ is only relevant to the fast group $g=1$ in region $C$, converts this to a set of four governing equations (which we refactor using removal cross-sections and diffusion length $L_g^2=\frac{D_g}{\SigRem}$):
\begin{align}
    \pd{\phi_{C1}}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{C1}^2}\phi_{C1} &= -\frac{S_{C1}}{D_{C1}}\label{eq:c1_govern}\\
    \pd{\phi_{C2}}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{C2}^2}\phi_{C2} &= -\frac{\Sigma_{sC1\rightarrow2}}{D_{C2}}\phi_{C1}\label{eq:c2_govern}\\
    \pd{\phi_{M1}}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{M1}^2}\phi_{M1} &= 0 \label{eq:m1_govern}\\
    \pd{\phi_{M2}}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{M2}^2}\phi_{M2} &= -\frac{\Sigma_{sM1\rightarrow2}}{D_{M2}}\phi_{M1}\label{eq:m2_govern}
\end{align}
\newcommand{\SigR}[1]{\Sigma_{R#1}}
As anticipated from earlier discussion, Eqs.~\ref{eq:c1_govern} and \ref{eq:m1_govern} are independent of other distributions, and can be solved first.
The flux $\phi_{C1}$ (core region $C$, fast group 1), with distributed source $S$, can be broken into homogenous and partial solutions:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \phi_{C1}=\phi_h + \phi_p\\
        \pd{\phi_h}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{C1}^2}\phi_h = 0\\
        \rightarrow\pd{\phi_h}{x}{2} = A_0\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}}) + A_1\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}})\\
        \pd{\phi_p}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{C1}^2}\phi_p = -\frac{S}{D_{C1}} \\
        \rightarrow\phi_p = L_{C1}^2\frac{S}{D_{C1}} = \frac{S}{\SigR{C1}} \\
        \rightarrow\phi_{C1} = A_0\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}}) + A_1\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}}) + \frac{S}{\SigR{C1}}
    \end{gathered}
    \label{eq:c1_flux}
\end{equation}
The other fast distribution $\phi_{M1}$, without such a source, simply reduces to a Helmholtz equation:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \pd{\phi_{M1}}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{M1}^2}\phi_{M1} = 0\\
        \rightarrow\phi_{M1} = A_2\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}^2}) + A_3\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}^2}) \\
    \end{gathered}
    \label{eq:m1_flux}
\end{equation}
These flux solutions can then be plugged into the governing equations for the low energy groups, and solving using viable particular and homogenous solutions:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \pd{\phi_{C2}}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{C2}^2}\phi_{C2} = -\frac{\Sigma_{sC1\rightarrow2}}{D_{C2}}\phi_{C1}\\
        \pd{\phi_{C2}}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{C2}^2}\phi_{C2} = -\frac{\Sigma_{sC1\rightarrow2}}{D_{C1}}\left(A_0\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}}) + A_1\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{C2}}) + \frac{S}{\SigRem}\right)\\
        \phi_{C2} = \phi_h + \phi_p\\
        \pd{\phi_h}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{C2}^2}\phi_h = 0\\
        \rightarrow\phi_h = A_4\cosh(\frac{x}{L_C2}) + A_5\sinh(\frac{x}{L_C2})\\
        \pd{\phi_p}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{C2}^2}\phi_p = -\frac{\Sigma_{sC1\rightarrow2}}{D_{C2}}\left(A_0\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}}) + A_1\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}}) + \frac{S}{\SigR{C1}}\right)\\
        \phi_p = -\frac{\Sigma_{sC1\rightarrow2}}{D_{C2}}\left(C_0\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}}) + C_1\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}}) + C_2x^2 + C_3x + C_4\right)\\
        \color{gray}C_0=A_0\frac{L_{C1}^2L_{C2}^2}{L_{C1}-L_{C2}}, C_1=A_1\frac{L_{C1}^2L_{C2}^2}{L_{C1}-L_{C2}}\\
        \color{gray}C_2=0,C_3=0,C_4=-\frac{SL_{C2}^2}{\SigR{C1}}\\
        \rightarrow\phi_p = -\frac{\Sigma_{sC1\rightarrow2}}{D_{C2}}\left(A_0\frac{L_{C1}^2L_{C2}^2}{L_{C1}-L_{C2}}\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}}) + A_1\frac{L_{C1}^2L_{C2}^2}{L_{C1}-L_{C2}}\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}})-\frac{SL_{C2}^2}{\SigR{C1}}\right)\\
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
\begin{multline}
    \rightarrow\phi_{C2}=A_4\cosh(\frac{x}{L_C2}) + A_5\sinh(\frac{x}{L_C2})\hfill\\\hfill-\frac{\Sigma_{sC1\rightarrow2}}{D_{C2}}\left(A_0\frac{L_{C1}^2L_{C2}^2}{L_{C1}-L_{C2}}\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}}) + A_1\frac{L_{C1}^2L_{C2}^2}{L_{C1}-L_{C2}}\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{C1}})-\frac{SL_{C2}^2}{\SigR{C1}}\right)
\label{eq:c2_flux}
\end{multline}
This process can then be repeated for the slow flux of region $M$:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \pd{\phi_{M2}}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{M2}^2}\phi_{M2} = -\frac{\Sigma_{sM1\rightarrow2}}{D_{M2}}\phi_{M1}\\
        \pd{\phi_{M2}}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{M2}^2}\phi_{M2} = -\frac{\Sigma_{sM1\rightarrow2}}{D_{M1}}\left(A_2\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}}) + A_3\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{M2}})\right)\\
        \phi_{M2} = \phi_h + \phi_p\\
        \pd{\phi_h}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{M2}^2}\phi_h = 0\\
        \rightarrow\phi_h = A_6\cosh(\frac{x}{L_M2}) + A_7\sinh(\frac{x}{L_M2})\\
        \pd{\phi_p}{x}{2} - \frac{1}{L_{M2}^2}\phi_p = -\frac{\Sigma_{sM1\rightarrow2}}{D_{M2}}\left(A_2\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}}) + A_3\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}})\right)\\
        \phi_p = -\frac{\Sigma_{sM1\rightarrow2}}{D_{M2}}\left(C_5\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}}) + C_6\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}})\right)\\
        \color{gray}C_5=A_2\frac{L_{M1}^2L_{M2}^2}{L_{M1}-L_{M2}}, C_6=A_3\frac{L_{M1}^2L_{M2}^2}{L_{M1}-L_{M2}}\\
        \rightarrow\phi_p = -\frac{\Sigma_{sM1\rightarrow2}}{D_{M2}}\left(A_2\frac{L_{M1}^2L_{M2}^2}{L_{M1}-L_{M2}}\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}}) + A_3\frac{L_{M1}^2L_{M2}^2}{L_{M1}-L_{M2}}\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}})\right)\\
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
\begin{multline}
    \rightarrow\phi_{M2}=A_6\cosh(\frac{x}{L_M2}) + A_7\sinh(\frac{x}{L_M2})\hfill\\\hfill-\frac{\Sigma_{sM1\rightarrow2}}{D_{M2}}\left(A_2\frac{L_{M1}^2L_{M2}^2}{L_{M1}-L_{M2}}\cosh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}}) + A_3\frac{L_{M1}^2L_{M2}^2}{L_{M1}-L_{M2}}\sinh(\frac{x}{L_{M1}})-\frac{SL_{M2}^2}{\SigR{M1}}\right)
\label{eq:m2_flux}
\end{multline}
These eight coefficients $A_0$ through $A_7$ can then be solved for using a set of eight boundary conditions: two reflection boundaries at $x=0$ for $\phi_{C1},\phi_{C2}$, two vacuum boundaries at $x=a+b$ for $\phi_{M1},\phi_{M2}$, and two conserservations each of flux and current (between $C$ and $M$ in groups 1 and 2), for a total of eight boundary conditions:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
    -D_{C1}\pd{\phi_{C1}}{x}{}(x=0)=0\\
    -D_{C2}\pd{\phi_{C2}}{x}{}(x=0)=0\\
    {\phi_{M1}}({x=a+b+2D_{M1}})=0\\
    {\phi_{M2}}({x=a+b+2D_{M2}})=0\\
    \phi_{C1}(x=a)=\phi_{M1}(x=a)\\
    \phi_{C2}(x=a)=\phi_{M2}(x=a)\\
    -D_{C1}\pd{\phi_{C1}}{x}{}(x=a)=-D_{M1}\pd{\phi_{M1}}{x}{}(x=a)\\
    -D_{C2}\pd{\phi_{C2}}{x}{}(x=a)=-D_{M2}\pd{\phi_{M2}}{x}{}(x=a)
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:bounds}
\end{equation}
By evaluating the flux solutions from Eq.~\ref{eq:c1_flux} through \ref{eq:m2_flux} at these boundary conditions, the coefficients can be found.
The line-by-line arithmetic of these evaluations is long and not of substantial intrinsic value, and is not included in this report for brevity.
The full coefficients have been evaluated separately using \texttt{Sympy}, and are used to evaluate final numeric values of this analytic solution.\cite{novak_computer_2026}

\section{Numerical Solution}
As became evident in the previous section, solving for analytic solutions to multigroup and multi-region problems, where the total number of governing equations to solve increases rapidly, managing analytic solutions can be quickly unwieldy in all but the most simple scenarios.
Instead, a common technique is to use finite-difference methods, where the derivatives of governing equations or boundary conditions are replaced with numerical approximations using the values at adjacent nodes.
For the discussed system in \figref{p1_setup}, without upscattering and with only the source in group 1:\\
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        - [\nabla\cdot D_1\nabla\phi_1]_i + (\Sigma_{a1i}+\Sigma_{s1\rightarrow 2i})\phi_{1i}= S_{1i}\\
        - [\nabla\cdot D_2\nabla\phi_2]_i + \Sigma_{a2i}\phi_{2i}= \Sigma_{1\rightarrow2i}\phi_{1i}\\
    \label{eq:multigroup-diff-nospace}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}



For the streaming term $-\pd{}{x}{}D\pd{\phi}{x}{}$, a finite difference approximation can be made using the two adjacent nodes\cite{OpenMC}:
\newcommand{\pfrac}{\left(\frac{D_{i+1}}{D_i+D_{i+1}}\right)}
\newcommand{\mfrac}{\left(\frac{D_{i-1}}{D_i+D_{i-1}}\right)}
\newcommand{\ddx}{\pd{}{x}{}}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:conserv-stream}
    \left[-\ddx D\ddx\phi\right]_i = -\frac{D_i}{h^2/2}\left(\pfrac\left(\phi_{i+1}-\phi_i\right)+\mfrac\left(\phi_{i-1}-\phi_i\right)\right) 
\end{equation}
This form of the equation is conservative, meaning it does not assume constant $D$ and can be used across material boundaries in space.
\newcommand{\dL}[1]{d_{L,#1}}
\newcommand{\dR}[1]{d_{R,#1}}
\newcommand{\dLR}[1]{d_{LR,#1}}

\renewcommand{\pfrac}{\left(\frac{D_{g,i+1}}{D_{gi}+D_{g,i+1}}\right)}
\renewcommand{\mfrac}{\left(\frac{D_{g,i-1}}{D_{gi}+D_{g,i-1}}\right)}
Inserting this form into Eq.~\ref{eq:multigroup-diff-nospace}, expanding each term and substituting shorthand $\dL{gi}=\frac{D_{gi}}{h^2/2}\mfrac$ and $\dR{gi}=\frac{D_{gi}}{h^2/2}\pfrac$ for the repeated fractions in Eq.~\ref{eq:conserv-stream}:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:shorthand}
    \begin{split}
        \dL{gi}&=\frac{D_{gi}}{h^2/2}\mfrac\\
        \dR{gi}&=\frac{D_{gi}}{h^2/2}\pfrac
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        -d_L\phi_{1,i-1} + \left(d_L+d_R+\Sigma_{R1,i}\right)\phi_{1,i} - d_R\phi_{1,i+1} = S_{1,i} \\
        -d_L\phi_{2,i-1} + \left(d_L+d_R+\Sigma_{a2,i}\right)\phi_{2i} - d_R\phi_{2,i+1} = \Sigma_{s1\rightarrow2,i}\phi_{1,i}
    \end{gathered}
    \label{eq:center-findiff}
\end{equation}
These equations apply to every of the $N+1$ nodes except for the leftmost $i=0$ and rightmost $i=N$, which lack such relative nodes $i-1$ and $i+1$, respectively.
Instead, at these points, we may apply the boundary conditions of $x=0$ and $x=a+b$.\\

At $x=0$, we apply the same symmetric boundary condition seen in Eq.~\ref{eq:bounds}, using a right-handed approximation of $\ddx\phi$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sym-findiff}
    \ddx\phi_{g,0} \approx \frac{3\phi_{g,0}-4\phi_{g,1}+\phi_{g,2}}{2h}=0
\end{equation}
Then, neglecting the extrapolated distance as in Eq.~\ref{eq:vac-bound}, no approximation of the derivative is necessary, and we simply set the final node's flux to zero:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:vac-findiff}
    \phi_{g,N} = 0
\end{equation}

By combining both flux distributions $\phi_{1,i}$ and $\phi_{2,i}$ (each with $N+1$ total nodes) into one distribution $\phi_i$ (with $2N+2$ nodes), such that references to any value $X\in\phi, \Sigma, \dots$ become $X_{1,i}\rightarrow X_i$ for group 1, and $X_{2,i}\rightarrow X_{i+(N+1)}$ for group 2, these series of equations for each of the $2N+2$ can be combined into one large matrix equation:

\newcommand{\downscat}[1]{\Sigma_{s1\rightarrow2,#1}}
\begin{landscape}
    \small
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -3 & 4 & -1 & & &   &&&&  \\
        -\dL{1} & \dLR{1}+\Sigma_{R,1} & -\dR{1} && &&&&\\
        & -\dL{2} & \dLR{2}+\Sigma_{R,2} & -\dR{2} & &&&&\\

Now consider again the same two-region device as in \figref{p1_setup}, but instead for a multiplying system rather than one with a distributed source $S$.
We shall consider two geometries of this multiplying system, with two different sets of multigroup properties.
As seen in Eq.~\ref{eq:solve-gxs}, multigroup properties must be evaluated with at least basic assumptions about the energy spectrum of the system, in order to have grouped properties which are self-consistent in solving that system.
Thus, for the two substantially different geometries considered in \figref{p2_setup}, it is not assumed that the spectra are fully comparable, and instead two different sets of group properties are identified and used.\\

\subsection{Theory}
Using multigroup diffusion theory to solve for these multiplying systems is similar in concept to non-multiplying multigroup diffusion.
As in the non-multiplying system, we consider a diffusion equation for each energy group $g$ using Eq.~\ref{eq:multigroup-diff}, but this time, retaining fission contributions instead of the distributed source:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{v}\pd{\phi_g}{t}{} - \nabla\cdot D_g\nabla\phi_g + \Sigma_{ag}\phi_g + \sum_{g'\neq g}^{G}\Sigma_{sg\rightarrow g'}\phi_g=\sum_{g'\neq g}^{G}\Sigma_{sg'\rightarrow g}\phi_{g'} + \chi_g\nu\sum_{g'}^{G}{\Sigma_{fg'}\phi_g'}
    \label{eq:fission-multigroup-diff}
\end{equation}
As with single group analysis, the time rate of change cannot immediately be ignored, and instead the equation is considered for a possible energy mode\cite{strong_npre_2026}, or relative flux distribution (i.e. independent of magnitude), and the fission multiplication term $\nu\Sigma_f$ scaled by $1/k$, to solve for the distribution independently of its time change.
The term $\chi_g$, retained in this analysis, represents the fraction of all fission neutrons that are born at this particular energy group.
Neutrons are typically born at very high energies from fission, so for an analysis using two groups, one may typically assume $\chi_1=1,\chi_2=0$, rather than including $\chi$ terms in the final equations.
Applying this assumption, alongside the assumption that no neutrons can upscatter from group 2 to 1, we obtain the following two-group governing equations for the multiplying region $C$:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        - \nabla\cdot D_{C1}\nabla\phi_{C1} + (\Sigma_{aC1}+\Sigma_{sC1\rightarrow 2})\phi_{C1}&= \frac{1}{k}\left(\nu\Sigma_{fC1}\phi_{C1} + \nu\Sigma_{fC2}\phi_{C2}\right)\\
        - \nabla\cdot D_{C2}\nabla\phi_{C2} + \Sigma_{aC2}\phi_{C2} -  \Sigma_{sC1\rightarrow2}\phi_{C1} &= 0\\
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:fission-twogroup}
\end{equation}
For the region $M$, with no fission cross-section, this reduces more simply:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        - \nabla\cdot D_{M1}\nabla\phi_{M1} + (\Sigma_{aM1}+\Sigma_{sM1\rightarrow 2})\phi_{M1}&= 0\\
        - \nabla\cdot D_{M2}\nabla\phi_{M2} + \Sigma_{aM2}\phi_{M2} -  \Sigma_{sM1\rightarrow2}\phi_{M1} &= 0\\
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:nothing-twogroup}
\end{equation}
As with the multiplying equation, we would solve for general solutions to these four governing equations, and introduce eight boundary conditions to precisely identify the final solutions.\\

For the distributions of flux in $M$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:nothing-twogroup}, which is non-multiplying, we could apply similar analytical procedure to the non-multiplying system discussed in the previous question.
However, for the $C$ region equations of Eq.~\ref{eq:fission-twogroup}, this procedure of identifying one solution first fails, as the two equations are now tightly coupled to each other, and we cannot choose one to solve first independently.
The specific algebraic techniques used to solve these tightly coupled differential equations are beyond the scope of this report, and examples may be found elsewhere.\cite{novak_lecture21_2026}\\
\subsection{Numerical Solution}
Instead, these governing equations will be used to construct a finite-difference solution from the start.
We approximate the streaming term similarly as to the non-multiplying systems in Eq.~\ref{eq:conserv-stream}, yielding similar governing finite-difference equations for body nodes:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        -d_L\phi_{1,i-1} + \left(d_L+d_R+\Sigma_{R1,i}\right)\phi_{1,i} - d_R\phi_{1,i+1} &= \frac{1}{k} \left(\nu\Sigma_{f1,i}\phi_{1,i}+\nu\Sigma_{f2,i}\phi_{2,i}\right)\\
        -d_L\phi_{2,i-1} + \left(d_L+d_R+\Sigma_{a2,i}\right)\phi_{2i} - d_R\phi_{2,i+1} - \Sigma_{s1\rightarrow2,i}\phi_{1,i} &= 0
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
As with the non-multiplying system, we then combine these equations into one flux distribution $\phi$ with $2N+2$ notes such that each value $X\in\phi, \Sigma, \dots$ becomes $X_{1,i}\rightarrow X_i$ for group 1, and $X_{2,i}\rightarrow X_{i+(N+1)}$ for group 2.
For the boundaries at $0, N, N+1, 2N+1$, as the boundaries remain symmetric on the left and vacuum on the right, we apply the same boundary conditions as in Eqs.~\ref{eq:sym-findiff} and \ref{eq:vac-findiff}.\\

However, unlike with the non-multiplying system, there are no independent sources to put into a constant matrix $b$, $A\phi=b$.
Instead, since we are solving for a multiplying system, we formulate a migration matrix $M$, and a fision matrix $F$ which contains the fission production terms all scaled by $\frac{1}{k}$ to form an equation $M\phi=\frac{1}{k}F\phi$, as seen in Eq.~\ref{eq:p2_mat}:

\newcommand{\fprod}[1]{\nu\Sigma_{f#1}}
\begin{landscape}
    \small
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -3 & 4 & -1 & & &   &&&&  \\
        -\dL{1} & \dLR{1}+\Sigma_{R,1} & -\dR{1} && &&&&\\
        & -\dL{2} & \dLR{2}+\Sigma_{R,2} & -\dR{2} & &&&&\\
        &&\ddots& \ddots & \ddots &&&&\\
        &&-\dL{N-1} & \dLR{N-1}+\Sigma_{R,N-1} & -\dR{N-1} &&&&\\
        &&&&1 &&&&\\
        0&&&& -3 & 4 & -1 & & &     \\
        &-\downscat{1}&&&&   -\dL{1+(N+1)} & \dLR{1+(N+1)}+\Sigma_{R,1+(N+1)} & -\dR{1+(N+1)} &&\\
        &&-\downscat{2}&&&&   -\dL{2+(N+1)} & \dLR{2+(N+1)}+\Sigma_{R,2+(N+1)} & -\dR{2+(N+1)} &\\
        &&\ddots& \ddots & \ddots &&\ddots& \ddots & \ddots\\
        && & -\downscat{2N} & &&-\dL{2N} & \dLR{2N}+\Sigma_{R,2N} & -\dR{2N}\\
        &&&&0 &&&&1 \\
    \end{bmatrix}&\\

        \phi_0\\\phi_1\\\phi_2\\\vdots\\\phi_{2N}\\\phi_{2N+1}
    \end{bmatrix}&\\
    \times \phi&\\
    =
    \frac{1}{k}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 &&&&         & 0&&&&         \\
        &\fprod{1}&&&  & &\fprod{N+2}&&&\\
        &&\fprod{2}&&  & &&\fprod{N+3}&&\\
        &&\ddots   &&  & &&\ddots     &&\\
        &&&\fprod{2N}& & &&&\fprod{2N} &\\
        &&&&0          & &&&&0          \\
        &&&&           & 0&&&&          \\
        &&&&           & &0&&&     \\
        &&&&           & &&0&&     \\
        &&&&           & &&\ddots&&     \\
        &&&&           & &&&0&     \\
        &&&&           & &&&&0     \\
    \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
        \phi_0\\\phi_1\\\phi_2\\\vdots\\\phi_{2N}\\\phi_{2N+1}
    \end{bmatrix}& \\
    =\frac{1}{k}F\phi&\\
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    \label{eq:p2_mat}
\end{landscape}

This equation, as with an analogous finite-difference model for single-group analysis\cite{strong_npre_2026}, forms an eigenvector system, as it is true for any (eigenvector) distribution, or mode, of flux $\phi$ (across both groups) which is scaled every generation by an (eigenvalue) multiplication rate $k$, and the dominant (largest) value of $k$ and corresponding distribution may be solved for by manipulating the relationship to return a new $\phi_{n+1}$ using a previous guess of $k_n$ and $\phi_n$ (where $n$ represents the index of power iteration):
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pow-iter}
    \begin{gathered}
        M\phi = \frac{1}{k}F\phi \\
        \phi = M^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{k}F\phi\right)\\
        \phi_{n+1} = M^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{k_n}F\phi_n\right)\\
        k_{n+1} = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^{2N+1}(\nu\Sigma_{f}\phi)_i}{\sum_{i=0}^{2N+1}(\Sigma_{a}\phi)_i}
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where the integral evaluations for $k_{n+1}$ are carried out using a numerical integral process such as a trapezoidal Reimann sum, over the full vector $\phi$ which corresponds to the flux across all nodes in both energy groups.
To maintain consistent scales of $\phi$, after every iteration the distribution is then normalized using a similar integral evaluation of neutron production, to normalize production to 1:
\begin{gather}
    P=\sum_{i=0}^{2N+1}(\nu\Sigma_{f}\phi)_i\\
    \phi = \frac{\phi}{P}
\end{gather}     

We implement a \texttt{Python} script which, starting from a reasonable initial guess of $k$ and $\phi$, repeats this power iteration process until reaching sufficient convergence criteria:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:convergence}
    \begin{gathered}
        \frac{||\phi_{n+1}-\phi_{n}||_2}{||\phi_n||_2} \leq 10^{-6}\\
        \frac{(k_{n+1}-k_n)^2}{(k_n)^2}\leq 10^{-6}
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}

The total (i.e across both energies) fission reaction rate at every point in space, for $a=25~\mathrm{cm}, b=25~\mathrm{cm},N=500$, may be observed in \figref{p2_rrate}.
In this plot, we observe that the majority of fission takes place in the core region adjacent to the moderator, where thermal neutrons (which have a high fission cross section, but are primarily formed in the moderator) enter the core region and are able to initiate fission.
There is contribution towards the center near $x=0$, from the fast neutrons, but this is minimal compared to the spike adjacent to the moderator.
If we were to use a one-speed model, we would not observe this phenomenon, often referred to as spatial self-shielding, and would instead observe a steady hump in reactions towards the center $x=0$ in the core, as the rate would only depend on where the highest total neutron flux is found, not where the thermal flux is specifically maximized in and near the moderator.
In our two-speed model, by contrast, we observe two distinct peaks in flux (\figref{fig:p2_fluxes}), one for fast flux in the core where fast neutrons are born through fission, and one for thermal flux in the moderator where thermal neutrons are born through downscattering.
This has a spatially self-shielding effect, where fast neutrons are born in the core, diffuse to the moderator, downscatter to thermal neutrons, and then reenter the core region, maximizing fission reaction rates in the core near the boundary where thermal neutrons (which much more readily trigger fission) enter from the moderator.

The solutions obtained using the finite-difference method may be in turn approximated using the infinite series of Legendre polynomials $L_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(x(\mu))=\sum_{i=0}^{m}C_iL_i(x)
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is a rescaled spatial variable ranging from 0 at $x=0$ to 1 at $x=a+b$, as Legendre polynomials take input on the domain $0<x<1$.
By evaluating some order $m$ of polynomials at each of the rescaled nodes of a numerical solution, and overfitting the numeric solution as a linear combination of each of the polynomials (in a matrix $A$), we can solve for a least-squares fit vector $\vec{L}$ of polynomial coefficients $L_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        A\vec{L}=\vec{\phi}\\
        \vec{L}=\left(A^TA\right)^{-1}A^T\vec{\phi}
    \end{gathered}
    \label{eq:leg-find}
\end{equation}
